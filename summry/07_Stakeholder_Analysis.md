## 🗺️ Big Picture

```
Any decision in business or technology affects more than just the decision-maker
        ↓
STAKEHOLDERS = anyone impacted by a decision
        ↓
Failing to identify stakeholders → making unethical decisions without realizing it
        ↓
Two frameworks for thinking about responsibility:
  ├── Friedman: maximize profit (shareholder-first)
  └── Porter: create shared value (society + shareholder)
        ↓
Both require ethical leadership to implement
```

## 1. Who are Stakeholders?

> **Stakeholder:** Anyone who is impacted by a decision-maker's decision.

### Corporate Stakeholder Examples

| Stakeholder | How they're impacted |
|-------------|---------------------|
| **Shareholders** | Financial returns; company value |
| **Employees** | Jobs, wages, working conditions |
| **Customers** | Product safety, price, data privacy |
| **Suppliers** | Payment, contracts, business stability |
| **Financiers** | Returns on loans/investment |
| **Families of employees** | Income stability, health benefits |
| **Local community** | Environmental impact, jobs, tax revenue |

> Stakeholders also include those **less directly related** to operations — e.g., future generations affected by environmental choices.

### Why Stakeholder Identification is Ethically Critical (from Slide 3)

> *"Identification of potential stakeholders is essential for ethical behavior, and failure to identify stakeholders has led many to make unethical decisions without ever realizing they had a moral dilemma in the first place."*

**Classic examples of unidentified stakeholders causing ethical failures:**
- **Profit vs. low wage:** A company maximizes profit by cutting wages → workers and their families are stakeholders whose interests were ignored
- **Production vs. pollution:** A factory increases output → neighboring community is a stakeholder whose health was ignored

### Uber as a Case Study (from Slide 4)
- One of Silicon Valley's biggest successes: ~$70B valuation, 70+ countries
- But: workplace culture described as "aggressive" and "unrestrained" (New York Times, 2017)
- **Ethical failure:** Leadership failed to identify employees' wellbeing as a stakeholder interest
- **Lesson:** Stakeholder analysis applies internally too — employees are stakeholders

## 2. Milton Friedman's View

> **"The social responsibility of business is to increase its profits."**  
> — Milton Friedman, *Capitalism and Freedom* (1962), Nobel Prize in Economics 1976

### What Friedman argued
- Businesses have **one primary responsibility:** generate returns for shareholders
- Spending company resources on social causes = a form of taxation without consent
- Economic freedom and political freedom are deeply linked

## 3. Michael Porter's Shared Value Creation

> Executives should NOT concentrate exclusively on profits. Instead, focus on **Creating Shared Value (CSV)**.

### What is Shared Value?

**Shared Value = creating economic value for shareholders WHILE creating social value by addressing society's needs and challenges.**

> This is a direct critique of Friedman: the premise is that economic and social value are not opposites — they can be pursued together.

### The Shared Value Creation Framework (from Slide 10)

**Dual focus:**
1. **Shareholder value creation** — profitability, competitive advantage
2. **Value creation for society** — addressing real social needs

**Real Example — GE's Ecomagination Initiative:**
- Provide cleaner and more efficient sources of energy
- Provide abundant sources of clean water anywhere in the world
- Reduce emissions
- **Result:** Both profitable AND socially beneficial

### Porter's Three Recommendations (from Slide 11)

| Recommendation | What it means | Example |
|----------------|---------------|---------|
| **1. Expand the customer base** | Bring in nonconsumers — including those at the "bottom of the pyramid" (BOP) | Microfinance for unbanked populations |
| **2. Expand traditional value chains** | Include nontraditional partners like NGOs, governments, academic institutions | Partnership with public health organizations |
| **3. Focus on regional clusters** | Create or support economic ecosystems that benefit a region | Silicon Valley; Electronic City (Bangalore); Chilecon Valley (Santiago) |

### Friedman vs. Porter — Side-by-Side

| | Friedman | Porter |
|--|---------|--------|
| **Primary goal** | Maximize profit | Create shared economic + social value |
| **View of social responsibility** | Not a business obligation | Core business strategy |
| **Stakeholder view** | Shareholders are primary | All stakeholders matter |
| **Ethical foundation** | Economic freedom as moral good | Social obligations are tied to business |
| **Real example** | Traditional shareholder returns | GE Ecomagination |

## 4. Business Ethics

> **Business Ethics** = an agreed-upon code of conduct in business, based on societal norms.

### What Business Ethics Does
- Lays the foundation for behavior consistent with principles, norms, and standards agreed upon by society
- Provides training for ethical decision-making in professional contexts
- Can differ across cultures — but some universal norms exist

### Universal Norms in Business Ethics (from Slide 13)
1. **Fairness** — equal treatment, no discrimination
2. **Honesty** — accurate representation, no deception
3. **Reciprocity** — mutual benefit, treating others as you'd want to be treated

## 5. Navigating Ethical Dilemmas in Business

### Three Questions to Ask (from Slide 14)

| Question | What it tests |
|---------|--------------|
| **"Does this fall within acceptable professional norms?"** | Are you acting within your industry's ethical standards and your organization's code of conduct? |
| **"Would I be comfortable explaining this publicly?"** | The transparency test — if you'd be embarrassed, that's a signal |
| **"How would the media report this?"** | Public accountability test — imagine it on the front page |
| **"How would stakeholders feel about it?"** | Stakeholder impact test |

## 6. Ethical Leadership

> *"Strategic leaders set the tone for the ethical climate within an organization."*

### Why Leadership Matters (from Slide 16)
- Employees take **cues from their environment** — if leaders cut ethical corners, employees follow
- CEOs of Fortune 500 companies are under **constant public scrutiny**
- Ethical standards must be the highest — unethical behavior destroys reputation
- **Ethical expectations must be clear** — ambiguity breeds rationalization

### The Uber Failure — Ethical Leadership Case Study
- Culture described as "aggressive" and "unrestrained"
- Leadership failed to model or enforce ethical standards
- **Consequences:** Public scandal, leadership changes, legal investigations, loss of trust

### Discussion from Slides — Facebook Dilemma
> *"Does Facebook have an ethical responsibility to curb the spread of misinformation and disinformation and provide corrections when people do share this content?"*

**Stakeholder analysis:**
- Facebook users — want protection from misinformation
- Advertisers — want engagement, which misinformation drives
- Society — harmed by misinformation at scale
- Facebook shareholders — concerned about regulatory risk

**Ethical frameworks applicable:**
- **Consequentialism:** Misinformation causes real societal harm → Facebook should act
- **Deontology:** Facebook has a duty as a platform to protect users
- **Stakeholder analysis:** Multiple stakeholders are harmed whose interests Facebook ignored
